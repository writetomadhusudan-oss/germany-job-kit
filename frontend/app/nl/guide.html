How Dutch hiring actually works

Dutch hiring is direct, informal, and English-friendly — especially in tech. Amsterdam, Eindhoven, Rotterdam, and Utrecht employ huge numbers of international IT professionals, and English is the working language at most multinationals (booking.com, ASML's suppliers, banks like ING and Rabobank, and the Amsterdam scale-up scene). English-only applications are accepted for most IT roles.

The dominant route for non-EU IT professionals is the Highly Skilled Migrant (kennismigrant) scheme: your employer must be an IND-recognised sponsor and your salary must clear a monthly threshold. Practically this means Dutch employers routinely sponsor foreign tech talent — sponsorship paperwork is standard, not a favour.

Timelines: 2–4 weeks from application to offer is common in tech. Notice periods in the Netherlands are typically 1–2 months, set by law or your contract.

The Dutch CV

  • 1–2 pages, reverse-chronological. Concise and factual — Dutch recruiters scan fast and dislike fluff.
  • Photo is optional. Many Dutch recruiters accept CVs without a photo; including one is fine but never required.
  • Never include: marital status, religion, date of birth is optional (most leave it out). Age discrimination rules mean personal data stays minimal.
  • Structure: Name + contact → Profiel (3–4 line summary) → Werkervaring (experience, quantified results) → Opleiding (education) → Vaardigheden (skills) → Talen (languages).
  • Language line matters: state your English level honestly and note Dutch if you're learning it — "Dutch A2, currently learning" signals commitment.

Motivation letters

  • Short and direct — under one page. The Dutch value "doe maar normaal" (just act normal): no flowery self-praise, no exaggeration.
  • Structure: your details, date, recipient, subject line, formal-but-warm greeting → 3–4 tight paragraphs (why the company, why you, your key qualifications, availability) → brief close.
  • Quantify results: "cut deployment time 40%" beats "experienced in deployments".

Where to apply

  • LinkedIn — the dominant channel for Dutch tech hiring.
  • Indeed.nl — high volume across sectors.
  • Nationale Vacaturebank — the Netherlands' largest Dutch-language job board.
  • Undutchables — jobs specifically for internationals (English-speaking roles).
  • Company career sites — ASML, Philips, ING, Booking.com, Adyen, Coolblue hire heavily direct.
  • Recruiters — firms like Huxley, Darwin Recruitment, and Adams Multilingual place a large share of Dutch IT roles.

HSM & EU Blue Card — the 2026 figures

  • Highly Skilled Migrant (kennismigrant) — employer must be an IND-recognised sponsor and files the application for you (fee €423 in 2026, ~2-week IND decision target). Salary thresholds (gross/month, excl. 8% holiday allowance):
    • 30 or older: €5,942
    • Under 30: €4,357
    • Reduced criterion (orientation-year / graduates within 3 years): €3,122
  • EU Blue Card — needs a contract of ≥6 months and a recognised higher-education degree; no recognised sponsor required. After 12 months on a Dutch Blue Card you gain intra-EU mobility. Thresholds (gross/month, excl. 8% holiday allowance):
    • Standard: €5,942
    • Reduced (degree within last 3 years): €4,754
  • The threshold in force is the one on your application date; thresholds update every January.

  • General information only — not legal advice. Verify at ind.nl.

The 30% ruling

  • A tax advantage for employees recruited from abroad: part of your salary is paid tax-free.
  • The full 30% rate still applies throughout 2026; it drops to a flat 27% from 1 January 2027.
  • 2026 minimum taxable wage: €48,013/year (≈ €68,590 gross). Under-30s with a non-Dutch master's: €36,497 taxable (≈ €52,139 gross).
  • Cap on the tax-free sum: €262,000; maximum duration 5 years.
  • Requires living 150+ km from the Dutch border for at least 16 of the prior 24 months.
  • Your employer applies for the ruling with the Belastingdienst — ask about it during salary negotiations.

Interviews

  • Expect 2–3 rounds: recruiter screen, hiring manager, technical/culture round. The Dutch are famously direct — interviews feel like conversations, not interrogations.
  • Directness goes both ways: asking blunt questions about salary, work-life balance, and the team is normal and expected.
  • Prepare examples with results; modesty is fine but underselling yourself reads as lack of confidence. Show, don't boast.
  • Dress is casual-to-smart — a suit is rarely needed outside banking and law. Match the company's vibe.
  • Always prepare 2–3 questions for them; "no questions" reads as disinterest.
